    Anthropic: every Claude model and the road to an IPO

    From Claude 4 to the Mythos-class models, a pause under US export controls, and a Nasdaq listing set for next week.

    In 30 seconds

    For everyone

    What happened

    Anthropic released Claude 4 in May 2025, then a new top model every few months, adding a 'Mythos' tier above Opus in 2026. It filed publicly for an IPO in October and began marketing its shares on 10 November.

    Why it matters

    Claude is one of the leading AI assistants, especially for coding and business use, and Anthropic's listing is a test of how investors value AI labs.

    What’s next

    Anthropic's shares closed their first day at $97.17, fell to about $88–89 on the second day and slipped again on 23 November, still above the $83 IPO price. A readable write-up of the Riemann proof is in draft; its key step has not yet been written out. Its first results as a listed company and the end of share lock-ups will be watched.

    23. 20 Nov 2026 (US time)2026
      20 Nov 2026 (US time) Tech New since your last visit Stated · not yet verified

      Anthropic mathematician claims a Riemann Hypothesis proof made with Claude; not yet verified

      An Anthropic mathematician announced on Friday that he believes he and Claude, Anthropic's AI model, have produced a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, a 167-year-old unsolved problem about how prime numbers are spread out, and of a wider version called the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, per IBM Think and Interesting Engineering. The argument was released mainly as computer-checkable code in the Lean proof language, with a four-page explanation. He credited Claude with 'rapid progress during the final stages'. The claim has not been peer reviewed, and independent mathematicians have yet to verify it. Fields Medal winner James Maynard told IBM Think: 'If this fully checks out, it is clearly a historic milestone in mathematics', adding that checking the Lean verification 'will take some time'. The problem is one of the Clay Institute's seven $1 million Millennium Prize Problems; Clay requires peer-reviewed publication and two years of scrutiny, so it remains officially unsolved.

    24. 21 Nov2026
      21 Nov 2026 Tech New since your last visit Reported · Lean and Mathlib community statement (via tech sites); not peer reviewed

      Lean and Mathlib communities certify the computer-checked Riemann proof; human review still to come

      The communities behind Lean, a computer system that checks mathematical proofs, and its main maths library Mathlib issued a joint statement saying the formal proof correctly states and proves the Riemann Hypothesis, per Unite.ai and Kingy AI. Dozens of people rebuilt the proof, and independent checking programs accepted it; one MIT mathematician reported that all 175,187 modules compiled with no errors. The statement covers only the computer-checked proof. There is no human-readable paper yet, and mathematicians still have to understand why the proof works; Terence Tao said digesting it 'will take some time'. The Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as unsolved: its $1 million prize requires publication, two years and broad acceptance.

    25. 21–22 Nov2026
      21–22 Nov 2026 Tech New since your last visit Reported · The Decoder; draft paper

      A readable draft of the Claude-assisted Riemann proof appears; it is unfinished and the key step is not yet written out

      A University of Waterloo mathematician posted a working draft of about 160 pages explaining the computer-checked proof in ordinary maths language. The draft says it was written by Claude, OpenAI's Codex and Google's Gemini at his request. It says it is unfinished: it does not yet prove the "main inequality", which it calls "the arithmetic heart" of the proof, and quotes it from the Lean code instead. The Decoder reports that mathematicians are still checking whether the Lean statement matches the Riemann Hypothesis exactly. A separate, human-written draft proof of a related conjecture, posted on Stanford professor Ravi Vakil's blog on 20 November, drew reports of problems within hours. Neither proof has been accepted.
