    US tariffs in 2026: struck down in court, rebuilt under new laws

    The Supreme Court threw out Trump's emergency tariffs in February. Washington has been rebuilding them, law by law, ever since. Here is where they stand.

    In 30 seconds

    For everyone

    What happened

    In February the US Supreme Court ruled that Trump's emergency tariffs were illegal. The US replaced them with a temporary 10% tariff, then in July with 10% or 12.5% tariffs on 60 economies. On 25 November it proposed further tariffs on 14 economies, up to 25% for India and 35% for Vietnam.

    Why it matters

    Tariffs are taxes paid by US importers on foreign goods. Higher rates make exports such as Indian textiles, gems and engineering goods dearer in the US, and can raise prices for US shoppers.

    What’s next

    Comments on the new proposal are due by 5 January 2027 and hearings begin on 25 January. No start date has been set; USTR Jamieson Greer said none of the new tariffs is likely to take effect for at least a couple of months. An appeal over the temporary 10% tariff is still pending.

    1. 20 Feb2026
      20 Feb 2026 Law New since your last visit Confirmed · US Supreme Court

      US Supreme Court rules the emergency-powers law does not allow tariffs

      In Learning Resources v. Trump, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), a law for national emergencies, does not give the President power to impose tariffs. That knocked out the 2025 'reciprocal' tariffs on most countries and the fentanyl-related tariffs on Canada, Mexico and China. Chief Justice Roberts wrote the majority opinion; Justices Thomas, Alito and Kavanaugh dissented, per ExFreight. Tariffs under other laws, such as the Section 232 duties on steel, aluminium and cars and the older Section 301 duties on China, were not affected. The court did not say how importers would get their money back. The President signed an order ending the IEEPA tariffs the same day.

    2. 24 Feb2026
      24 Feb 2026 Trade New since your last visit Confirmed · White House proclamation

      A temporary 10% tariff on almost all imports replaces the struck-down duties

      A 10% surcharge on imports from all countries took effect at 12:01 am US Eastern time, under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974. That law lets a President add up to 15% for no more than 150 days unless Congress extends it. The order was signed on 20 February, the day of the court ruling. Many goods were exempt, including energy, some farm products, pharmaceuticals, some electronics, cars and goods already covered by Section 232 tariffs, per Troutman Pepper Locke. Collection of the old IEEPA tariffs stopped from the same moment.

    3. 11 Mar2026
      11 Mar 2026 Trade New since your last visit Official · USTR

      US opens 'excess capacity' investigations into 16 economies

      The US Trade Representative (USTR) opened Section 301 investigations into whether 16 economies have built far more factory capacity than they can use, flooding world markets with cheap goods. They are China, the EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Bangladesh and Mexico. Section 301 lets the US respond to trade practices it finds unfair, and unlike Section 122 it has no time limit. Hearings ran from 5 to 8 May. USTR later said it heard from nearly 140 witnesses and received 1,070 comments.

    4. 20 Apr2026
      20 Apr 2026 Policy New since your last visit Reported · ExFreight, citing CBP

      US customs opens a system to refund the struck-down tariffs

      US Customs and Border Protection opened the first phase of CAPE, a system for importers to claim back IEEPA tariffs, after the Court of International Trade ordered it to recalculate duties without them. Only the company that imported the goods can claim, not shoppers who paid higher prices. CBP says valid refunds are generally paid 60 to 90 days after a claim is accepted. By 11 September, about $134.7 billion in refunds had been accepted and about $122 billion sent to the Treasury for payment, according to CBP figures cited by ExFreight.

    5. 7 May2026
      7 May 2026 Law New since your last visit Ruled · under appeal

      Trade court rules the temporary 10% tariff unlawful, but most importers keep paying

      The US Court of International Trade ruled 2–1 that the Section 122 surcharge went beyond what the law allows. The majority said the law is meant for specific balance-of-payments problems, not the trade deficits the order cited, per Skadden. Relief applied only to the three plaintiffs: Washington state and two small importers. Everyone else kept paying. The government appealed the next day, and the appeals court paused the ruling while it hears the case, per Skadden and ExFreight.

    6. 24 Jul2026
      24 Jul 2026 Trade New since your last visit Confirmed · USTR

      New 10% or 12.5% 'forced labour' tariffs on 60 economies replace the temporary tariff

      As the 150-day Section 122 tariff ended, new Section 301 tariffs took effect on imports from 60 economies. USTR based them on findings about forced labour. Nineteen economies, including India, Canada, Mexico, the UK, Bangladesh and Indonesia, face 10%. Forty-one, including China, Vietnam, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, face 12.5%. For the EU and Taiwan the 10% is a combined cap that includes normal duties, and for Japan, South Korea and Switzerland the cap is 12.5%. Goods covered by Section 232 and qualifying goods from Canada and Mexico under their trade pact are exempt. USTR says the tariffs affect 99.4% of US imports, per Sandler, Travis & Rosenberg.

    7. 25 Nov2026
      25 Nov 2026 Trade New since your last visit Proposed · USTR

      US proposes new tariffs on 14 economies: up to 25% for India, 35% for Vietnam

      USTR Jamieson Greer found that 14 of the 16 economies investigated since March have unfair excess-capacity practices, and proposed new Section 301 tariffs. The proposed rates are up to 35% for Vietnam, 25% for India, 20% for China and Singapore, 18% for Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand, and 15% for the EU, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland. Several would be lower with a trade deal: India 17%, Vietnam 20%, Singapore 10%. The rates are 'net of' existing Section 301 tariffs, meaning they count the July duties rather than simply adding to them. Some goods would be exempt; it is not yet clear whether medicines are among them, per Becker's Hospital Review. No decision was made on Mexico or Norway. Nothing takes effect yet: comments are due by 5 January and hearings start on 25 January, and Greer said final regulatory steps must be completed first.

    8. 25 Nov2026
      25 Nov 2026 Trade New since your last visit Stated · USTR Jamieson Greer (to CNBC)

      Greer says the proposed tariffs are unlikely to start for at least a couple of months

      US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told CNBC's Squawk Box: "I don't think any of these would actually take effect earlier than a couple months from now." He said that for most of the 14 economies the new rates would "top up a few percentage points" once existing duties are counted. Stocktwits reports Greer said China is an exception: the roughly 25% first-term duties on about two-thirds of Chinese imports would stay, with the proposed 20% added on top, while about $30 billion of Chinese goods would be exempt. Greer also said the US is "sticking to the deals" it has, naming China, Thailand, India and the EU. India's commerce ministry said India "does not have structural excess capacity in any sector."

    What this means for you

    If you work for an exporter or own shares in export-heavy companies, the final US rate matters more than the proposal. Nothing proposed on 25 November applies yet, and rates can change after hearings or trade deals.
